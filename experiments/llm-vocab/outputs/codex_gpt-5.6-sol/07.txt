A database index is like the index at the back of a book. Instead of reading every page to find a topic, you look it up in the index and jump directly to the relevant pages.

Without an index, a database may need to scan every row in a table to answer a query. With an index on a column—such as `email`—the database maintains an additional, ordered data structure that helps it locate matching rows much faster.

Indexes improve searches, sorting, joins, and uniqueness checks. However, they have costs: they consume storage and must be updated whenever rows are inserted, changed, or deleted. Too many indexes can therefore slow down writes.

Good candidates for indexing include columns frequently used in `WHERE`, `JOIN`, and `ORDER BY` clauses. Columns with very few distinct values, such as a boolean field, often benefit less. The goal is balance: index the access patterns that matter, not every column.
