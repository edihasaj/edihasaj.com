Learning to cook began less like a skill and more like a negotiation with failure. My first attempts were tentative and overly literal—I measured everything, followed recipes to the letter, and still managed to burn garlic or oversalt a sauce. What changed things wasn't a single breakthrough but repetition: making the same dish enough times that my hands started knowing what my eyes hadn't yet learned to see, like the exact moment onions turn from raw to sweet.

Eventually cooking stopped feeling like following instructions and started feeling like conversation—tasting, adjusting, trusting a little improvisation. I began to understand why a recipe called for resting dough or searing before braising, not just that it did. There's a quiet confidence that comes from that shift, a sense that mistakes are just data rather than failures. Now cooking is one of the few things I do that rewards patience so directly and immediately: the reward is dinner, and the lesson sticks a little more each time I get it wrong.
