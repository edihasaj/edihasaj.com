Learning to cook began, for me, as a matter of necessity rather than passion. Takeout was expensive, and the novelty of instant noodles wore thin fast. My first attempts were clumsy: onions burned while I searched for the next ingredient, pasta turned to mush, and I once mistook a tablespoon of salt for a teaspoon with predictable results. But somewhere between the failures, I started noticing small things: how garlic smells sweet just before it turns bitter, how a pinch of acid can rescue a flat sauce, how heat is a tool you learn to read rather than fear.

What surprised me most was how cooking reshaped my relationship with time and attention. Recipes became suggestions instead of commandments. I stopped measuring everything and started tasting. Cooking taught me patience through simmering, improvisation through empty pantries, and generosity through feeding others. It is still the most reliable way I know to turn an ordinary evening into something worth remembering.
